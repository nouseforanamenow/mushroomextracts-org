How to Choose a Mushroom Supplement: The Complete Buyer's Guide

The mushroom supplement market lacks independent oversight, leaving consumers confused about quality, extraction methods, and authenticity. This comprehensive guide cuts through marketing claims to help you identify genuine, effective mushroom extracts based on scientific standards, third-party testing, and labeling transparency that separates premium products from mediocre alternatives.

Introduction: Why This Guide Exists — The Testing Gap in Mushroom Supplements

The dietary supplement industry generates over $150 billion annually, yet mushroom extracts remain one of the least regulated categories. Unlike pharmaceutical products subject to FDA oversight, mushroom supplements can reach market with minimal quality assurance. The result: a landscape filled with misleading labels, counterfeit beta-glucan claims, and products that deliver fraction of their promised bioactive compounds.

Mushroom Extracts was created to fill this gap. As an independent review site, we don't sell products, hold manufacturer relationships, or receive commissions. We analyze labels, review certificates of analysis, and evaluate manufacturing practices using the same rigorous standards that established pharmaceutical companies follow. This guide synthesizes our methodology and testing insights to empower you as a consumer.

What you'll learn: the critical label information that indicates quality, red flags that signal inferior or fraudulent products, how different extraction methods affect potency, how to interpret certificates of analysis, and the testing standards that separate legitimate manufacturers from opportunistic sellers.

What to Look For on Labels: The Critical Information

Quality mushroom supplement labels clearly identify the mushroom species by scientific name, specify fruiting body or mycelium sourcing, disclose extraction method and solvent type, list beta-glucan percentage, declare country of origin, and display third-party certifications. The presence or absence of these details immediately signals whether a manufacturer prioritizes transparency or obscures production methods.

1. Mushroom Species Name (Common AND Scientific)

A legitimate label lists both the common name and scientific name. For example: "Lion's Mane (Hericium erinaceus)" not just "Lion's Mane." This matters because common names vary by region. In China, several Hericium species are sold interchangeably, but only Hericium erinaceus has robust clinical evidence for cognitive support.

Many manufacturers skip the scientific name to hide substitution. A product labeled only "Reishi" could contain Ganoderma lucidum (legitimate) or Ganoderma sichuanicum (less effective). The scientific name removes ambiguity and indicates the manufacturer understands—and respects—the botanical classification of their ingredient.

2. Fruiting Body vs. Mycelium: The Most Important Distinction

This is the single most misrepresented aspect of mushroom supplements. Fruiting bodies are the visible mushroom cap and stem. Mycelium is the root-like network underneath. Fruiting bodies contain 3-5x higher beta-glucan concentrations than mycelium, making them the superior source for medicinal mushrooms.

Manufacturers prefer mycelium because it's cheaper and faster to produce (30-40 days vs. 90-180 days for fruiting bodies). Many label mycelium-based products as "whole mushroom" or omit the distinction entirely, hoping consumers won't notice. A transparent label explicitly states: "Fruiting Body Extract" or "Mycelium Extract."

Red flag: Labels using vague language like "mushroom complex," "mycelium biomass," or "mushroom powder" typically indicate mycelium with lower bioactive content. Some manufacturers sell mycelium grown on grain substrates (rice, oats), which inflates the weight and lowers the effective mushroom content—a practice we address in the red flags section below.

3. Extraction Method: Water, Alcohol, Dual, or Other

Extraction method determines which bioactive compounds end up in your supplement. Different mushrooms have different soluble compounds. The most common methods are water extraction, alcohol extraction, dual extraction (sequential water and alcohol), and nanofiber processes.

A transparent label states the extraction method clearly. For example: "Dual Extract: Fruiting Body (water and alcohol extracted)" tells you exactly what you're getting. The absence of this information suggests the manufacturer either uses a simplistic process or wants to obscure their method.

Learn more about extraction methods in our detailed section below. For now, understand that method disclosure indicates the manufacturer understands bioactive chemistry and isn't hiding an inferior process.

4. Beta-Glucan Percentage or Bioactive Content Claims

Beta-glucans are polysaccharides responsible for most of the immune-modulating effects associated with medicinal mushrooms. Quality mushroom extracts list beta-glucan percentage, typically ranging from 20-50% in fruiting body extracts and 5-15% in mycelium extracts.

How that number is measured matters. A credible beta-glucan figure comes from an enzymatic assay, for example the Megazyme mushroom and yeast beta-glucan method, which measures beta-glucan specifically and separates out alpha-glucans such as starch from grain. A generic "polysaccharides" figure, often from a colorimetric test, can include those starches and other sugars and overstate the active content, so a high polysaccharide number is not the same as a high beta-glucan number.

If a label doesn't list beta-glucan percentage and doesn't reference testing documentation, you have no way to verify potency. Manufacturers avoid this disclosure when their products don't meet therapeutic thresholds. For premium extracts, expect labels to state: "Contains 30% beta-glucans (verified by third-party testing)."

Some brands use trademarked names for bioactive compounds (like "LEM" for Lentinula edodes mycelium) without quantifying active content. While not automatically fraudulent, this approach allows them to make claims without substantiation. Specific percentages backed by testing are always preferable.

5. Country of Origin: Where the Mushroom Was Grown

Country of origin matters because growing conditions—temperature, humidity, substrate, and harvesting methods—affect bioactive compound development. Most medicinal mushrooms are grown in Asia (primarily China, India, and Vietnam), and that's perfectly fine if quality controls exist.

What matters: the label should clearly state where the mushroom was grown. "Cultivated in China" or "Grown in India" provides traceability. Some premium brands specify even more detail: "Handpicked Fruiting Bodies from the Yunan Province, China." This level of specificity allows you to research growing conditions and manufacturer reputation.

Red flag: Labels that say "Product of [Country]" but don't specify where the mushroom was grown—only where it was bottled—often obscure foreign sourcing. An ethical manufacturer is transparent about both cultivation and processing locations.

6. Third-Party Certifications and Testing Badges

Legitimate certifications include USP (United States Pharmacopeia), NSF Certified for Sport, ConsumerLab verification, and various organic certifications (USDA Organic, EU Organic). These organizations conduct independent testing and verify label claims.

Not all badges are equal. Certifications from established organizations like USP or NSF carry weight. In-house testing labeled as "third-party verified" is suspicious—true third-party testing uses independent labs with no financial relationship to the manufacturer.

Look for specific certification logos on the label and verify them on the certifying organization's website. Fraudulent badges are surprisingly common, and manufacturers sometimes claim certifications they don't actually have. Spending 60 seconds to verify authenticity often reveals significant quality gaps.

Key Takeaway: Label Transparency Checklist

  • ✓ Species name (both common and scientific)
  • ✓ Fruiting body or mycelium (explicitly stated)
  • ✓ Extraction method (water, alcohol, dual, etc.)
  • ✓ Beta-glucan % (or documented bioactive content)
  • ✓ Country of origin (where mushroom was grown)
  • ✓ Third-party certifications (verifiable through official channels)
  • ✓ Certificate of Analysis (available upon request or on website)

Products missing 3 or more of these elements warrant skepticism.

Red Flags in Mushroom Supplements: What to Avoid

Common warning signs include proprietary blends that hide ingredient amounts, vague labels using "mushroom powder" instead of extract terminology, missing certificates of analysis, unrealistic health claims, and prices suspiciously lower than competitors. These red flags indicate either cost-cutting measures, deliberate deception, or both.

Red Flag 1: Proprietary Blends Without Disclosed Percentages

A label listing "Proprietary Mushroom Blend (500mg)" without specifying how much Lion's Mane, Reishi, or other mushrooms are included prevents you from verifying effective doses. This structure allows manufacturers to use minimal amounts of expensive mushrooms and pad the blend with cheaper fillers.

Example: A "Proprietary Immune Blend" might contain 400mg of grain filler (from mycelium on grain substrate) and only 100mg of actual medicinal mushroom. Without knowing the breakdown, you have no way to assess potency. Quality manufacturers list each ingredient with its specific amount: "Reishi (Ganoderma lucidum) Fruiting Body Extract 250mg, Lion's Mane (Hericium erinaceus) Fruiting Body Extract 250mg."

Red Flag 2: "Mushroom Powder" Instead of "Extract"

This terminology signals a major quality difference. "Mushroom powder" means dried and ground whole mushroom with minimal processing. "Extract" means the bioactive compounds have been concentrated. An extract of the same weight as powder delivers 3-5x more active compounds.

Many manufacturers use "mushroom powder" because it's cheaper to produce and can be labeled with higher serving sizes to appear more potent. A 1500mg serving of mushroom powder sounds better than 300mg of extract, but the extract is the superior product. Terms like "mushroom complex," "mushroom biomass," and "freeze-dried mushroom" fall into this category—they're not extracts and have lower bioactive concentrations.

Red Flag 3: No Certificate of Analysis Available

A certificate of analysis (COA) is a document from a third-party lab showing exactly what compounds are in the product. It verifies beta-glucan percentages, screens for contaminants, and confirms the mushroom species through testing.

If a manufacturer doesn't provide a COA—claiming it's "proprietary" or "too technical for consumers"—they're either hiding poor results or don't test their products. Legitimate manufacturers publish COAs on their websites or provide them upon request. The absence of testing is a serious red flag for quality and authenticity.

Red Flag 4: Unrealistic or Unsubstantiated Claims

Avoid products making claims like "cures cancer," "reverses aging," "eliminates anxiety," or "replaces pharmaceutical medications." While medicinal mushrooms have genuine benefits supported by research, these claims exceed what the evidence shows and violate FDA regulations on supplement marketing.

Legitimate claims reference the mechanism: "Supports immune function" (backed by beta-glucan research), "Promotes mental clarity" (supported by Lion's Mane studies), "Aids relaxation" (Reishi research). Manufacturers making exaggerated claims are prioritizing sales over accuracy and likely cutting corners elsewhere.

Be especially wary of products claiming to contain "activated" bioactive compounds or proprietary formulations that "outperform" standard extracts without providing research or testing data to support these claims. Marketing language without substantiation is a hallmark of lower-quality manufacturers.

Red Flag 5: Prices Suspiciously Lower Than Competitors

Mushroom extracts cost $15 to $50 per bottle at retail, where a bottle is usually about a one-month supply. A bottle listed at $5 to $8 (from an unknown brand) warrants skepticism. You're likely looking at mycelium on grain substrate rather than fruiting body extract, or a diluted product with minimal active compounds.

This doesn't mean all affordable products are inferior—some established manufacturers achieve economies of scale that allow competitive pricing. But paired with missing COAs, vague labels, or proprietary blends, extremely low pricing is a red flag suggesting corner-cutting.

Red Flag 6: Mycelium on Grain Without Clear Disclosure

Some manufacturers grow mycelium on grain substrates (rice, oats, barley), which is cheaper and faster than fermentation-based mycelium production. The final product can contain 50%+ grain by weight. While not inherently harmful, this practice is rarely disclosed and artificially inflates serving sizes.

A product labeled "500mg per capsule" might contain only 200mg of mycelium and 300mg of grain filler. Ethical manufacturers disclose this: "Mycelium on Rice Bran (200mg mycelium, 300mg substrate)." Absent this transparency, assume the manufacturer is hiding dilution.

Extraction Methods Decoded: How Process Affects Potency

Different extraction methods target different bioactive compounds: water extraction dissolves polysaccharides and beta-glucans, alcohol extraction isolates terpenes and triterpenes, dual extraction combines both, and nanofiber processing enhances bioavailability. The best method depends on which mushroom species and which therapeutic compounds matter most for your intended use.

Water Extraction

What it is: Mushroom material is simmered in water at high temperatures for hours to days. Water dissolves polysaccharides (including beta-glucans), which are responsible for immune modulation. The liquid is then evaporated to concentrate the bioactive compounds.

Pros: Water extraction is traditional, effective for polysaccharide-rich mushrooms like Reishi and Shiitake, requires no chemical solvents, and leaves no residual alcohol. It's suitable for people avoiding alcohol.

Cons: Water doesn't dissolve triterpenes (found in Reishi) or other fat-soluble compounds as effectively. The extraction is incomplete compared to dual extraction. Additionally, heating can degrade some heat-sensitive compounds, though this is typically minimal with proper temperature control.

Alcohol (Ethanol) Extraction

What it is: Mushroom material is soaked in ethanol (typically 40-95% concentration) for weeks to months. Alcohol dissolves compounds that water doesn't: triterpenes, sterols, and fat-soluble bioactives. The ethanol is then evaporated, leaving behind a concentrated extract.

Pros: Alcohol extraction captures triterpenes (crucial for Reishi's calming effects) and other compounds water misses. It's highly effective for mushrooms where these compounds are therapeutic. Alcohol also acts as a natural preservative, extending shelf life without additives.

Cons: Some people prefer to avoid alcohol, though the final product contains minimal residual ethanol. Alcohol extraction doesn't capture beta-glucans as efficiently as water extraction. For the full spectrum of mushroom benefits, alcohol extraction alone is incomplete.

Dual Extraction (Sequential Water + Alcohol)

What it is: Water and alcohol extractions are performed sequentially (water first, then alcohol, or vice versa). Both processes are preserved and combined into a single extract that captures both water-soluble and alcohol-soluble compounds.

Pros: Dual extraction is the most comprehensive method, capturing the full spectrum of bioactive compounds from a mushroom. You get beta-glucans, triterpenes, sterols, and other therapeutic compounds in one product. This is the gold standard for medicinal mushroom supplements and reflects how traditional herbal medicine prepares mushrooms.

Cons: Dual extraction is more expensive and time-intensive than single-method extraction. It requires more mushroom material and more processing. The final product may contain trace amounts of residual ethanol, though reputable manufacturers ensure evaporation is complete. Cost is the main limiting factor—expect dual extracts to command premium pricing.

Nanofiber and Bioavailability-Enhanced Processing

What it is: Emerging technologies that further break down mushroom cell walls and bioactive compounds into smaller, more absorbable particles. This can include nanofiber technology, fermentation pre-processing, or enzymatic treatment. The goal: increase the percentage of compounds your gut actually absorbs.

Pros: Significantly improved bioavailability means you absorb more of the beneficial compounds. Nanofiber processes can increase absorption by 30-50% compared to standard extracts. For expensive mushrooms like Lion's Mane, this translates to needing smaller doses for the same effect.

Cons: These technologies are newer and less standardized across the industry. Claims of improved bioavailability should be backed by published research or third-party testing. The cost is higher than conventional extraction. Early research is promising, but long-term safety and efficacy data is still accumulating.

Method Beta-Glucans Triterpenes Cost Best For

Alcohol Moderate Excellent Low Reishi, Lion's Mane

Which method should you prioritize? Dual extraction is the most comprehensive and reflects traditional herbal preparation methods. If cost is a primary concern, a high-quality alcohol extract of Reishi or water extract of Shiitake is preferable to a proprietary blend powder. Emerging nanofiber technologies show promise but should be backed by published research specific to the product you're considering.

Understanding Certificates of Analysis: How to Read and Interpret COAs

A Certificate of Analysis is a third-party laboratory report documenting the exact composition, potency, and safety of a mushroom supplement. It verifies beta-glucan percentages, confirms species authenticity through DNA testing, screens for heavy metals and microbial contaminants, and lists analytical methods used. Learning to read a COA gives you objective proof of product quality.

Key Sections of a Certificate of Analysis

1. Product Identification and Lab Information

The COA should clearly identify the product by name and lot number, the manufacturer/submitter, the testing lab (with name and accreditation status), and the testing date. The lab should be accredited by organizations like A2LA (American Association for Laboratory Accreditation) or equivalent international bodies. This verification ensures the lab meets published testing standards.

Red flag: A COA from an in-house or unaccredited lab, or one that doesn't list the lab's credentials, isn't truly independent. Look for accreditation numbers or certificates of recognition from recognized accrediting bodies.

2. Potency Testing: Beta-Glucan and Bioactive Content

The most important section for mushroom supplements. This shows the percentage of beta-glucans (or other bioactives) in the finished product. Reputable fruiting body extracts should show 20-50% beta-glucans. Mycelium extracts typically show 5-20%. If the COA shows lower percentages than claimed on the label, that's a major red flag.

The COA should specify which analytical method was used (HPLC, UV-Vis, etc.). Different methods can yield slightly different results, but the method should be clearly documented so you know how the number was derived. Some labs test beta-glucans by weight; others use molecular methods. Understanding the methodology helps you compare across different products and labs.

3. Species Authentication (DNA Testing)

Advanced COAs include DNA barcode testing, which confirms the mushroom species matches the label. This is crucial because substitution and adulteration are common in imported mushroom materials. A COA stating "DNA barcode: 100% match to Hericium erinaceus" confirms you're getting exactly what the label promises.

Without DNA testing, you're relying on the manufacturer's claims about sourcing. DNA barcoding is the gold standard for species verification and increasingly available from reputable testing labs.

4. Contaminant Screening: Heavy Metals, Microbials, and Pesticides

The COA should test for:

Heavy metals: Lead, cadmium, arsenic, and mercury. Mushrooms are bioaccumulators—they absorb heavy metals from soil and water. The FDA has set limits for supplements: Lead <0.1 ppm, Cadmium <0.1 ppm (though these are typically stricter for mushrooms). The COA should show results well below these thresholds or confirm "not detected."

Microbial contaminants: Tests for E. coli, Salmonella, mold, and other pathogens. Results should show "not detected" or be below detection limits. The testing standard used should be documented (USP, FDA BAM, etc.).

Pesticide residues: Screening for commonly used agricultural pesticides. Results should show either "not detected" or be below established residue limits.

A comprehensive COA tests all three categories. If a COA is missing any of these, ask the manufacturer why and request supplementary testing from an accredited lab.

How to Spot a Fake or Questionable COA

No accreditation mentioned: The lab should list its accreditation status. Unaccredited labs have no external oversight ensuring they follow proper testing protocols.

Too-perfect results: COAs showing zero detected contaminants across all tests are suspiciously clean, especially for products imported from regions with known environmental challenges. Real-world testing typically shows trace amounts of common substances. Impossibly clean results suggest testing wasn't performed or was manipulated.

Proprietary or unlisted methods: If the COA doesn't specify the analytical method used or says "proprietary method," question how the numbers were derived. Standard methods are documented and reproducible—manufacturers shouldn't hide them.

Generic format: A COA that looks like a template with identical formatting across multiple products and manufacturers is likely fabricated. Each lab and each testing run produces unique, detailed results.

Questions to Ask Manufacturers About Their COAs

If a manufacturer provides a COA but you have questions:

  • → "What lab tested this product?" Request the lab's name and contact info. Verify the lab's accreditation independently.
  • → "Is this lot-specific?" Every batch should have its own COA. If a manufacturer shows the same COA for multiple products or batches, that's a red flag.
  • → "Does the potency match your label claim?" If the label says "30% beta-glucans" and the COA shows 18%, there's a discrepancy worth investigating.
  • → "When was this tested?" A COA from 2 years ago doesn't guarantee current batches match those results. Request current testing or batch-specific COAs.
  • → "Was species identity confirmed by DNA testing?" If not, ask why and whether the lab offers this service.

Testing and Quality Standards: GMP, Third-Party Testing, and Regulatory Compliance

Quality mushroom supplements are manufactured under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards, independently tested by accredited third-party labs, and comply with FDA dietary supplement regulations. These standards ensure consistent potency, purity, and safety. Understanding which certifications and standards matter helps you distinguish established manufacturers from new or questionable producers.

Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) Certification

GMP is a system of quality controls enforced by the FDA for supplement manufacturing. Facilities certified under GMP follow strict protocols for sourcing ingredients, controlling manufacturing processes, testing finished products, and maintaining documentation. A GMP-certified facility doesn't guarantee quality, but it ensures minimum standards are met.

There are two types of GMP certifications relevant to mushroom supplements:

FDA GMP: The manufacturer's facility has been inspected by the FDA and found to comply with Good Manufacturing Practice regulations. This is the legal baseline for supplement manufacturing in the US. Most legitimate manufacturers have this certification.

cGMP (Current Good Manufacturing Practice): A third-party auditor (ISO-accredited) has verified that the facility meets or exceeds FDA GMP standards. cGMP certification is a higher standard and indicates the manufacturer invested in external validation. Look for cGMP certification if available—it's a good indicator of seriousness about quality.

Red flag: If a manufacturer doesn't mention GMP certification and has never been inspected by the FDA, there's minimal assurance that manufacturing practices are controlled and documented.

Third-Party Testing and Certification Programs

Beyond GMP, several third-party organizations conduct independent testing and certification of supplement products:

USP (United States Pharmacopeia) Verified

USP is a gold-standard certification in the supplement industry. USP-verified products have been independently tested for label accuracy, purity, and potency. The manufacturer must demonstrate that the product contains what the label claims, is free from contaminants, and meets strict quality standards. If you see "USP Verified" on a mushroom supplement, it's a strong indicator of quality.

NSF Certified for Sport

NSF testing is commonly used by manufacturers targeting athletes and fitness-conscious consumers. NSF-certified products are tested for label accuracy and screened for banned substances. While excellent for sports supplements, NSF certification is less common for medicinal mushroom products than USP.

ConsumerLab.com Approved

ConsumerLab conducts independent testing of supplements and publishes results online. Products that pass their testing are listed on the ConsumerLab website with detailed test results. This is a valuable resource—you can look up specific products and see their testing data directly.

Organic Certifications (USDA Organic, EU Organic, etc.)

Organic certification verifies that mushrooms were grown without synthetic pesticides, herbicides, or GMO practices. For mushroom supplements, organic certification adds value because it reduces the risk of pesticide residues. However, organic certification doesn't guarantee higher potency or superior extraction methods—it only addresses growing practices.

Heavy Metal Limits and Contaminant Standards

The FDA doesn't set enforceable limits for heavy metals in supplements, but industry standards exist. Most reputable manufacturers follow these thresholds:

Industry Standard Heavy Metal Limits

Heavy Metal Typical Limit Health Risk
Lead < 0.1 ppm Neurological damage, developmental issues
Cadmium < 0.1 ppm Kidney damage, bone disease
Arsenic < 0.5 ppm Cancer risk, organ damage
Mercury < 0.1 ppm Neurological damage, kidney disease

ppm = parts per million. Products from reputable manufacturers should test well below these limits or show "not detected."

Mushrooms naturally accumulate metals from soil, especially if grown in regions with industrial pollution or acid rain. A comprehensive COA should test for all four metals and show results below these thresholds. Products from manufacturers that don't test for heavy metals are a significant concern, especially if the mushroom source is not verified.

Microbial and Mold Testing Standards

Mushroom supplements should be tested for microbial contamination and mold. Standard tests include:

Total Aerobic Microbial Count (TAMC): Measures overall bacterial and fungal content. Industry standard: <100,000 CFU/g (colony-forming units per gram) for herbal products.

Pathogenic organisms: Specifically tests for E. coli, Salmonella, Listeria, and other food-borne pathogens. Results should be "not detected."

Mold and yeast: Tests for Aspergillus and other toxic molds. Some regions have stricter limits due to aflatoxin risks. Results should be "not detected" or well below safety limits.

A quality COA will document all these tests and show results meeting or exceeding these standards.

Price vs. Quality Analysis: Why Cheap Isn't Always Bad, and Expensive Isn't Always Good

Quality mushroom extracts range from $15 to $50 per bottle at retail. Prices below $8 usually indicate mycelium or grain-diluted products; prices above $40 may reflect branding rather than superior extraction. Value lies in verified potency, third-party testing, and transparent sourcing—not marketing or fancy packaging. A moderately priced dual extract with published COA outperforms an expensive proprietary blend.

Understanding the Cost Structure of Mushroom Supplements

Several factors legitimately affect the price of mushroom supplements:

Source material cost: Fruiting body extracts cost significantly more than mycelium-based products because fruiting bodies are more expensive to produce and require higher-quality sourcing. A premium Lion's Mane fruiting body extract costs more than a Reishi extract because Lion's Mane production is more resource-intensive.

Extraction method: Dual extraction costs 30-50% more than single-method extraction because it requires more processing time and mushroom material. Nanofiber technology and bioavailability enhancements add additional costs.

Testing and certification: Third-party COAs, USP verification, and organic certification add 15-25% to production costs. Manufacturers willing to bear these costs are generally more quality-focused.

Brand and marketing: Established brands with consumer recognition command premium pricing. This isn't inherently bad—reputation is built on consistent quality—but marketing costs are reflected in the price.

Supply chain and sourcing: Direct partnerships with trusted farms, transparent supply chains, and ethical labor practices cost more than spot-market purchasing. Manufacturers paying premium prices for verified, high-quality sourcing pass that cost to consumers.

Conclusion: Applying These Standards to Your Supplement Selection

The mushroom supplement market is evolving. More manufacturers are adopting transparent labeling, pursuing certifications, and publishing testing data. But mediocre and fraudulent products still dominate retail shelves and online marketplaces. Your job as a consumer is to demand the standards outlined in this guide.

When evaluating a mushroom supplement, ask these questions:

  1. Is it fruiting body or mycelium? Look for explicit disclosure. Fruiting body is superior.
  2. What's the extraction method? Dual extraction is ideal; single-method extraction is acceptable if appropriate for the mushroom.
  3. Is there a published COA? Non-negotiable. Request it if not on the website.
  4. Do the claims match the testing? If the label says "30% beta-glucans" but the COA shows 15%, that's a red flag.
  5. Are there third-party certifications? USP, NSF, or ConsumerLab verification adds confidence.
  6. Is the pricing reasonable? Compare per-serving costs across similar product types, but don't sacrifice testing and transparency for lower prices.

Our evaluations are based solely on objective criteria: label transparency, third-party testing, sourcing quality, and extraction methodology. Our mission is to empower consumers with the knowledge needed to make informed choices in a complex and often opaque market.
